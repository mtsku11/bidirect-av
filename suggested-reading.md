# Suggested Reading and Research Notes for SLITSCAN.AV

This document collects the suggested reading and research threads mentioned in our discussion so far. It is intended as a practical grounding document for the project as it moves toward:

1. building the instrument,
2. testing and documenting its behaviour,
3. situating it in existing research, and
4. writing a paper for NIME.

---

## Core reading order

These are the first texts to read closely.

### 1. Kiefer / Eldridge / Overholt (2025)
**Future Research Challenges in Feedback Musicianship**

**Why read it first**
- Best entry point for the current feedback-musicianship landscape.
- Helps frame the project as an instrument with feedback behaviour, not just an effect.
- Useful for identifying language around playability, stability, agency, liveness, and emergent behaviour.

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- The planned reciprocal feedback layer sits very close to this discourse.
- Useful for motivating why controllable feedback between modalities is a research question.

**What to extract into notes**
- Definitions of feedback musicianship.
- Identified open problems or research challenges.
- Vocabulary for describing performer–system interaction.

---

### 2. Sanfilippo & Valle (2013)
**Feedback Systems: An Analytical Framework**

**Why read it early**
- Foundational theoretical grounding for feedback systems in music and sound art.
- Helpful for avoiding vague use of the word “feedback.”

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- Supports precise framing of reactive routing vs reciprocal closed-loop feedback.
- Gives a conceptual language for talking about internal system behaviour, coupling, and system structure.

**What to extract into notes**
- Core typologies or analytical distinctions.
- Any language useful for describing loop structure and system boundaries.
- Concepts that apply to cross-modal coupling, even if the original focus is not audiovisual.

---

### 3. Eldridge / Kiefer / Overholt / Ulfarsson (2021)
**Self-resonating Vibrotactile Feedback Instruments**

**Why read it**
- Concrete, worked example of a feedback-based instrument.
- Good bridge between abstract theory and actual instrument design.

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- Not cross-modal audiovisual in the same sense, but very relevant in terms of instrument behaviour, tuning, damping, and performer interaction.
- Useful for thinking about how instability becomes musical rather than merely broken.

**What to extract into notes**
- How the instrument is described technically.
- How feedback is constrained and made playable.
- What kinds of evaluation or observations are reported.

---

### 4. Di Scipio & Sanfilippo (2019)
**Defining Ecosystemic Agency in Live Performance**

**Why read it**
- Strong philosophical and conceptual framing for systems with distributed or shared agency.
- Helpful when describing the instrument as collaborator rather than obedient tool.

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- Especially relevant once reciprocal feedback is added.
- Supports discussion of autonomy, co-behaviour, and system self-evolution.

**What to extract into notes**
- Definitions of agency.
- Claims about performer, system, and environment.
- Language for describing a live system that is partly self-directing.

---

### 5. Levin (2000)
**Painterly Interfaces for Audiovisual Performance**

**Why read it**
- Foundational AV instrument / performance-system reference.
- Historically important for audiovisual performance framing.

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- Grounds the project in the lineage of audiovisual instruments rather than only feedback systems.
- Useful for discussing mapping, liveness, and simultaneous shaping of image and sound.

**What to extract into notes**
- How the audiovisual instrument is framed.
- What design principles are foregrounded.
- How performance is positioned relative to interface and media.

---

### 6. Memo Akten’s PhD-era / deep visual instrument work
**Deep Visual Instruments and related work**

**Why read it**
- Closest adjacent line if the project later moves toward learned latent spaces or ML-mediated control.
- Useful contrast case.

**Why it matters for SLITSCAN.AV**
- Helps clarify that your current approach is explicit, analysis-driven, and performer-facing rather than latent-space-driven.
- Useful to cite as adjacent but distinct.

**What to extract into notes**
- Where latent-space control differs from explicit signal-analysis routing.
- Whether performer control is direct, learned, inspectable, or opaque.
- How audiovisual generation and interaction are framed.

## Secondary lineage and contextual references

These are not necessarily the first things to read in depth, but they help position the project historically and conceptually.

### Cybernetic / feedback / live electronic music lineage

#### David Tudor — *Rainforest* series
**Why it matters**
- Canonical example of acoustic-electronic feedback and distributed behaviour.
- Helpful when thinking about instruments as systems with internal dynamics.

#### Gordon Mumma — *Hornpipe*
**Why it matters**
- Strong precedent for performer–electronics reciprocity.
- Useful when framing feedback as an active relationship rather than a one-way effect.

#### Pauline Oliveros — Expanded Instruments System
**Why it matters**
- Important lineage for delay, feedback, and instrument expansion over time.
- Helps contextualise temporal extension and performer co-adaptation.

### Video / visual feedback lineage

#### Steina and Woody Vasulka
**Why it matters**
- Important for feedback-rich audiovisual and video-processing lineages.
- Useful visual-side historical grounding.

#### Sandin Image Processor / Rutt-Etra Scan Processor
**Why it matters**
- Explicit modular image processing and feedback histories.
- Strong lineage for a project that treats image memory and signal routing as instrument material.

---

## Adjacent format-blending and audiovisual instrument references

These help situate SLITSCAN.AV in broader image–sound translation and hybrid media instrument histories.

### ANS synthesizer (Murzin)
**Why it matters**
- Important precedent for drawing / image-to-sound relationships.
- Useful when discussing cross-domain thinking and shared representational spaces.

### Xenakis — UPIC
**Why it matters**
- Canonical graphical sound composition system.
- Strong precedent for cross-modal structuring of sound and image-like representations.

### MetaSynth / Photosounder
**Why it matters**
- More recent software lineage for image–sound and spectrogram-based thinking.
- Helpful for distinguishing your project from image-to-sound translation tools.

### Aphex Twin / spectrogram-hidden imagery references
**Why it matters**
- Cultural, not strictly academic.
- Useful as public-facing inspiration, but should not substitute for scholarly grounding.

Example already mentioned in discussion:
- **Co-constructing a Dual Feedback Apparatus**

**Why it matters**
- Signals that feedback, agency, and recursive behaviour are live contemporary concerns.
- Helps ensure the paper does not claim a total vacuum where there is emerging adjacent work.

**How to use it**

## Working research claim supported by this reading

A careful version of the project’s research positioning could be:

> There is an under-explored area around real-time, performance-grade audiovisual instruments that use explicit analysis-driven cross-modal routings, especially when those routings are made reciprocally feedback-coupled and controllable at the per-routing level.

This phrasing is preferable to saying that no related work exists.

## What to look for while reading

For each source, make notes under the same headings so later paper writing is easier:

- **Domain** — feedback musicianship, AV instrument, psychophysics, ML instrument, cybernetic art, etc.
- **System type** — reactive, closed-loop, generative, latent-space, physical, software, installation, instrument.
- **Real-time?**
- **Performance-oriented?**
- **Cross-modal?**
- **Analysis-driven?**
- **Explicit routing control?**
- **Feedback-coupled?**
- **What is the performer actually controlling?**
- **What is the system controlling on its own?**
- **Most useful quote / concept / term**
- **How SLITSCAN.AV is similar**
- **How SLITSCAN.AV is different**

## Recommended note-taking outputs

As you read, build three living documents alongside the project:

### 1. `literature-matrix.md`
A comparison grid of relevant works with columns such as:
- real-time,
- audiovisual,
- feedback,
- explicit routing,
- performer control,
- evaluation style,
- relevance to your contribution.

### 2. `research-claims.md`
A short file containing:
- candidate contribution claims,
- evidence for each claim,
- papers that support or weaken each claim,
- wording that feels safe for a NIME submission.

### 3. `quotes-and-concepts.md`
A file of useful terms, definitions, and properly attributed quotations to revisit when writing the paper.

## How this reading should feed the project

### For the build
The reading should help determine:
- which routings are central enough to prototype first,
- how to describe damping, leak, and stability,
- what counts as “instrument behaviour” rather than just “effect behaviour.”

### For testing
The reading should help determine:
- whether evaluation is framed as usability, performability, agency, emergence, or phenomenology,
- what kinds of observations belong in logs and performer notes,
- what failure modes matter academically.

### For the paper
The reading should help answer:
- What tradition is this project in?
- What exact gap does it address?
- What is novel here: the mapping design, the reciprocal feedback architecture, the performer-facing control design, the browser-native implementation, or the evaluation findings?

Read the six core sources first and produce a one-paragraph note for each answering:

1. What does this source say?
2. What does it contribute to my framing?
3. What does it not cover that SLITSCAN.AV might cover?

That will give you enough grounding to begin writing a serious related-work section while the technical implementation continues.
